Christian Borough Project · Household guide

Where is the manual?

What would a family actually do?

This page is for a household weighing whether to join, strengthen, or start a Christian borough. It is not a relocation pitch, a political mobilization page, or another directory. No community is listed here as having openings; you bring your own discernment and your own relationships.

What we mean by “borough”

A borough, as we use the term, is a local community of Christian households, anchored by a church, oriented toward useful work in the same place. People know one another. They worship together, raise children in the same parish or congregation, trade skills and goods, and carry ordinary civic duties where they live.

That is different from a faction: a borough does not require hostility toward other faithful churches or families, and it does not treat every disagreement as treason. Healthy boroughs can cooperate with neighboring believers without merging identities or surrendering conscience.

What a family actually does

Most of borough life is unglamorous and repeatable:

  • Worship weekly in a congregation you can accountable to, not merely visit.
  • Choose housing you can sustain on honest income, near the people and church you depend on.
  • Work productively — employed, self-employed, or serving — so the household is not a burden on the community.
  • School and form children deliberately (home, Christian school, or public with eyes open).
  • Practice hospitality and mutual aid without pretending you are a commune.
  • Stay legible to civil authorities where conscience allows — taxes, zoning, contracts, and neighborliness.
  • Keep an exit plan if leadership fails, doctrine drifts, or the place becomes unsafe.

Tools that already exist (public)

We do not invent partner pages on this draft. Use these verified public resources to research place, church, commerce, and magistrates:

  • RESOLUTE Citizen scorecard Research local, state, and federal officials by evidence-backed scores — who holds office where you may live.
  • Church directory (usmcmin.org) Find congregations and related ministry data on the .org site. Verify worship and doctrine in person.
  • Christ-centered commerce directory Fredericksburg-area businesses scored on a public rubric. Useful as a pattern for local work and trade; not a national borough listing.

Confirmed facts vs. claims that need evidence

Treated as established here

  • Households need a real church, real work, real housing, and real accountability — not a brand.
  • Public tools can help you research; they cannot replace vows, membership, or trust earned over time.
  • This site publishes draft guides for review before any push to production.

Not stated here as fact

  • Fixed deadlines (for example, “two years left”) for national collapse or resettlement.
  • Broad allegations of election fraud or stolen outcomes without case-specific, cited proof.
  • Predictions that a place or nation will inevitably fail by a certain date.

If someone urges haste using those claims, slow down. Ask for primary sources. A borough decision should survive even if the headline cycle moves on.

Household decision checklist

Work through this together — husband and wife first, then older children as appropriate. Print or save; check items only when you can name a concrete answer, not a hope.

Questions a receiving community must answer

Before a family commits, the receiving borough (or church-led fellowship) should answer in writing where possible. If they cannot answer plainly, treat that as data.

  1. Church relationship — Is membership in a specific congregation required? What if we disagree with a non-essential?
  2. Housing — What realistic options exist in the next 90 days? Any strings attached (informal “debt” to leaders)?
  3. Work — What employers, trades, or introductions do you offer? What is aspirational vs. confirmed?
  4. Children — What schooling do most families use? Any pressure to isolate children from relatives or outsiders?
  5. Healthcare — Nearest competent care for emergencies and pregnancy?
  6. Commerce — Where do families routinely buy food, goods, and services? Any exclusivity demands?
  7. Leadership — Who makes binding decisions? Terms, recall, and finances — where are records?
  8. Doctrine and discipline — Statement of faith, membership vows, and recent discipline cases (summarized, not gossip).
  9. Conflict history — Major splits or lawsuits in the last decade? Honest account preferred.
  10. Exit — If we leave, will relationships and reputation be sabotaged? What commitments unwind cleanly?

What websites and AI cannot do

AI assistants, scorecards, directories, and polished pages can organize information and surface public sources. They cannot take vows for you, raise your children, sit in your elders’ meeting, or guarantee that a community keeps its promises. Community is made by persons under God — in worship, work, confession, forgiveness, and years of showing up. Treat every online tool as a map, not the territory.
