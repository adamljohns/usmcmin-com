Defense News

The captain of the Coast Guard Cutter Storis says the ship's first full Arctic deployment proved the icebreaker can run law-enforcement boardings, live-fire gunnery drills and even refuel a Navy destroyer — missions the service hadn't attempted in the high north in decades

Capt. Corey Kerns told Defense News the Storis spent 133 days around the Arctic Ocean and Alaska this year, during which Coast Guard R&D Center and Pentagon Defense Innovation Unit staff tested aerial drones from Textron and Skyfront for ice navigation and station-keeping. The ship, a retrofitted commercial icebreaker, carried out the first law-enforcement mission by a polar icebreaker in more than two decades, boarding fishing vessels in the Bering Sea with NOAA's law-enforcement office, and ran a mock refueling of a national security cutter that Kerns said shows Storis could save a Navy destroyer a week and a half of transit time if it needed fuel in the Arctic. The crew also spent some 300 hours monitoring four Chinese research vessels that hadn't registered their activity, at one point lacking secure comms with headquarters in Juneau and Alameda — a gap Kerns said has since been improved. The deployment frees the aging icebreaker Healy, sidelined twice by mechanical trouble, to focus on science missions ahead of the Coast Guard's first purpose-built icebreaker around 2033.

Political

New Jersey lawmakers are considering what would be the first state bill in the nation to define 'Islamophobia' in law, and a Heritage Foundation analysis argues the measure is written so broadly it would function as a blasphemy law barring honest evaluation of Islam and its culture

The Heritage Foundation piece argues the bill is not merely descriptive of anti-Muslim bigotry but so overbroad in its definition that it would make it practically impossible to evaluate Islam and the culture it produces in any meaningful or critical way, raising First Amendment concerns about a government-enforced definition of religious criticism.

Heritage Foundation

Victorian-era scholar-diplomat James Bryce's century-old warnings about demagoguery are drawing fresh attention after this month's Republican midterm losses, with a Providence essay arguing American democracy is faltering for the same reasons Bryce diagnosed in Britain before World War I

The essay draws on Bryce's 'The American Commonwealth' and 'Modern Democracies' to argue healthy democracy requires a shared 'sense of common interest' that elites earn through 'energy and intellectual eminence' and that an educated public cultivates through 'constant discussion.' It contends America's divisions — from 2024's immigration rhetoric to 2026's populist anger at oligarchy — show that sense of common interest has eroded alongside the decline of public colleges, print journalism and civic education, and points to campus 'civics centers' and Texas governor-elect Gina Hinojosa's pledge to reinvest in public schools as rare grounds for optimism.

Providence

Francis Fukuyama's new memoir has commentators revisiting his famous 'End of History' thesis, and a First Things essay argues he gives too little weight to what Kierkegaard said religion alone supplies: recognition from God rather than an endless, often violent competition for recognition among men

The essay traces Fukuyama's Hegelian argument that liberal democracy's promise of equal recognition (isothymia) can leave unsatisfied the human drive for more-than-equal recognition (megalothymia), risking a self-destructive 'Last Man' nihilism. It argues Kierkegaard saw further: that seeking recognition from 'the crowd' — even an elite crowd — 'incarnates untruth,' and that only recognition from God, as in St. Paul's 1 Corinthians 9 image of a disciplined runner serving all rather than mastering all, lets a person be fully known and redirects the competitive impulse toward nonviolent ends.

Tech / AI

German public broadcasters report an intelligence assessment that Russia could launch a nuclear-armed satellite dubbed Sputnik-S within 60 days, a weapon that would violate the 1967 Outer Space Treaty and that NATO chief Mark Rutte says the alliance is actively guarding against

An investigation by German broadcasters WDR and NDR and the newspaper Süddeutsche Zeitung reports the satellite has been assembled and stored near Moscow for several years, with an early-November intelligence assessment saying it could launch within 60 days. Rutte, asked about the report in Berlin alongside Chancellor Friedrich Merz, said NATO is 'constantly assessing' its deterrence posture, including 'the space element.' Defense News notes a nuclear detonation in orbit could disable large numbers of satellites and cripple power grids via electromagnetic pulse, pointing to the 1962 U.S. Starfish Prime test, which damaged the Telstar-1 satellite launched the day after. The report also recalls then-Rep. Mike Turner's 2024 warning of a 'serious national security threat' and Western suspicion that Russia's 2022 Kosmos-2553 satellite was a dummy used to test how radiation affects satellites; the Russian Embassy in Berlin called the reporting a 'propaganda campaign' and said its space activities are 'exclusively for peaceful purposes.'

Defense News

Japanese auto-parts makers are pivoting into defense work as Tokyo encourages its automotive supply chain to join the country's biggest military buildup since World War II, with one motor supplier's defense revenue growing to a tenth of sales after it started building parts for Anduril drones

Defense News reports Aster, a company that long supplied Toyota, now powers Anduril-built drones and has seen defense work grow since entering the sector last year, with CEO Takenobu Hongo saying 'drones are a much better business' than unprofitable auto motors. Japan's procurement agency ATLA is matching suppliers like Aster with arms firms and new funding streams, backed by an advisor to Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's Cabinet, as the country's auto industry — which built nearly 900,000 fewer vehicles in 2025 than a decade earlier — looks to diversify. Toyota, Honda and Nissan have so far resisted entering defense directly, citing reputational risk and business ties to China, but smaller suppliers such as engine-maker Kyowa Industrial and electronics giant Mitsubishi Electric are moving into drone components and ventures.

Culture

The Museum of the Bible's newly opened Bowden Gallery of Biblical Art launches with 'The Annunciation: Picturing the Incarnation of Christ,' a 22-piece exhibit spanning a 1493 Nuremberg Chronicle illustration to Andy Warhol and Salvador Dali's takes on the moment Gabriel told Mary she would bear the Son of God, WORLD reports

WORLD's dispatch highlights Bruce Herman's 2006 gold-leafed panel 'Overshadowed,' depicting Mary staring up in wonder, and a Delft tile from the Calvinist Netherlands that curator Amy Van Dyke says once adorned a fireplace because 'it brought religious imagery into the home where it was OK to do because it was a functional thing.' The exhibit's centerpiece is a life-sized installation by artist Theodore Prescott showing a plaster-cast woman startled by a neon-light outline of an angel filling the gallery with warm red light. The show opened Nov. 16 and runs six months, through April 5, 2027 — the Feast of the Annunciation — before a new collection replaces it.

WORLD

'If Jefferson was wrong, America is wrong,' an 1874 biographer wrote — a Law & Liberty essay revisits that claim on the Declaration's semiquincentennial, tracing how Jefferson's reputation has risen and fallen with America's own crises from the Civil War through Charlottesville

The essay argues Jefferson didn't 'invent' America so much as successive generations invented 'Jefferson,' invoking him because he located legitimate authority in the people themselves rather than in a king or a single founding father like Washington. It traces his reputation's swings — a champion of religious liberty in the 1920s, an object of civil-rights-era reckoning over the roughly 600 people he enslaved and his children by Sally Hemings, and a flashpoint again after 2017's Charlottesville violence and a 2021 New York City Council vote to remove his statue — concluding his idea of 'generational sovereignty,' that each generation must earn and preserve self-government anew, remains 'a hopeful prediction, a provocation, perhaps a prayer' for a nation still questioning whether it is one people.
