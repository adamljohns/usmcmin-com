Defense News

Two Navy F-35C Lightning II fighters completed the jet's first launch and recovery testing aboard the future USS John F. Kennedy, clearing the way for the Navy to operate the stealth fighter from every carrier in its fleet once the ship is fully equipped

Lockheed Martin said the two jets, assigned to Air Test and Evaluation Squadron 23, flew 34 flights over 11 days of at-sea testing that began Oct. 13, logging 122 catapult launches and 122 arrested landings aboard the Ford-class carrier. Unlike the steam catapults and hydraulic gear on Nimitz-class ships, Kennedy uses the Electromagnetic Aircraft Launch System and Advanced Arresting Gear, and a Lockheed official told Defense News the Navy has now cleared the F-35C for operations fleetwide, though ships still need their launch and recovery bulletins published and supporting equipment installed before they can deploy the jet.

Political

A new book from three national-security experts says China, Russia, Iran and North Korea have deepened an informal 'axis of aggressors' through the 2020s, and warns Washington still lacks a coherent strategy to counter their growing cooperation

The book, 'Axis of Aggressors,' by Bradley L. Bowman, Elaine K. Dezenski and retired Rear Adm. Mark Montgomery of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies, argues the four authoritarian regimes have taken advantage of American weakness to deepen cooperation across multiple domains, even without a formal treaty like Nazi Germany's alliance with Fascist Italy and Imperial Japan. A review in Providence says the authors don't offer silver-bullet fixes but give readers a clearer picture of the dangers facing American interests and a handful of solutions the piece argues should draw bipartisan support, at a moment when Washington's foreign-policy establishment mostly debates how to manage decline rather than reverse it.

Providence

Social Security's retirement promises are pitting living retirees against the future taxpayers financing them, a new argument for gradual reform says, proposing to shield older workers nearing retirement while moving younger workers toward privately owned retirement accounts

The Law & Liberty essay applies Edmund Burke's view of society as a partnership between the dead, the living and the unborn to Social Security's Old Age and Survivors Insurance program, arguing the system's designers built in incentives for politicians to promise benefits while organized retirees defend transfers financed by younger and future workers. It proposes a Burke-and-Buchanan approach — cohort separation that protects those with little time left to adjust while gradually moving younger workers toward privately owned retirement savings — as a politically feasible way to honor obligations to today's elderly without permanently locking in the incentives that keep passing the bill forward.

Law & Liberty

This month's lopsided Republican losses — 30 House seats and at least seven Senate seats — show most Americans swinging back toward the same policies they rejected only two years earlier when they elected Trump, a new commentary says

The piece argues voters were swayed by Democrats blaming the Iran war and high grocery and fuel prices on Republicans without offering workable fixes, pointing to Biden-era inflation and the high cost of living in blue states as evidence that the same tax-and-spend policies would deepen Americans' economic pain if applied nationally. It frames the swing as evidence of a deeper discernment problem among American voters rather than a single policy failure, arguing Christians should read the results as a signal of where the nation's values actually stand rather than take comfort in Trump's 2024 win as a lasting turn.

Tech / AI

The U.S. burned through a third to two-thirds of key long-range missile stockpiles fighting Iran, a new defense-industry analysis finds, warning the military may have to lean on shorter-range munitions until production catches up

The Center for Strategic and International Studies report, using munitions-expenditure data current as of Nov. 3, found Patriot, THAAD and Standard Missile interceptors most depleted with no substitutes available, while longer-range strike weapons like Tomahawks, JASSM cruise missiles and Precision Strike Missiles are heavily used but have some substitutes. The report concludes the U.S. still has enough munitions for a renewed fight against Iran but faces 'great concern' against a peer threat like China, and says rebuilding stockpiles despite boosted manufacturer production rates and possible congressional funding will take a multiyear commitment beyond the current crisis.

Defense News

The Netherlands unveiled a defense-industrial plan targeting 40 percent growth and about $16.5 billion in annual industry revenue by 2030, backed by roughly $1.8 billion in government spending on missile defense, autonomous systems and military sustainment

The Dutch government's plan funds integrated air and missile defense, data-driven command systems and digital resilience, unmanned platforms, and sustainment, with about $660 million earmarked for scaling industrial production, $350 million for international cooperation and $165 million to harden supply chains. The Netherlands is NATO Europe's seventh-largest defense spender but lacks a prime comparable to Sweden's Saab or Germany's Rheinmetall, though firms like Thales's Dutch radar operations and missile-maker Destinus — whose Ruta cruise missiles Ukraine used against the Kerch Strait bridge this week — give Amsterdam outsized niche strength the government says it wants to scale alongside its own armed forces' needs.

Culture

Booker Prize-winning novelist M. John Harrison's new novel 'The End of Everything' tells its post-apocalyptic story through deliberately opaque, screen-like prose that works better on the page than it would on film, a new review argues

First Things' review follows Philip Tennent, a 38-year-old Englishman who recovers one of the novel's 'artefacts' — amorphous sea-born objects that slowly take on human appearance and behavior after an unexplained force called the iGhetti disrupts life across Britain — as he and his elderly aunt travel through fading Kentish beach towns. The reviewer argues Harrison's prose, which 'simulates description as often as it describes,' exploits how much language can leave unshown in a way cinema's visual exposition cannot replicate, making the novel a rare case where the written word outperforms the screen that science fiction usually favors.

First Things

A wave of apps now commodifying friendship — Bumble BFF, the mom-focused Peanut, and the AI-chat app Caeli — echoes a darker endpoint Kazuo Ishiguro imagined years before them in his novel 'Klara and the Sun,' now adapted for film

The Public Discourse piece traces a string of real apps that turn friend-finding into a swiping game or AI-chat subscription, arguing that even though commodifying friendship once seemed less likely to catch on than commodifying dating, it has followed the same path. It reads Ishiguro's novel — in which lonely, genetically 'lifted' children are given solar-powered robot 'artificial friends' to ease isolation caused by at-home schooling and social fragmentation — as a warning about what happens when technology is asked to substitute for the real, costly work of human friendship rather than support it.
