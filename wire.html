A Victorian-era British ambassador to Washington who wrote the industrial-age successor to Tocqueville's Democracy in America offers an unsettling lens on this month's midterm result, a new essay argues -- warning that demagoguery and a decayed public opinion, not any single law, are what let democracies rot

Providence turns to James Bryce, the Belfast-born scholar-diplomat who served as British ambassador to Washington under Teddy Roosevelt and authored The American Commonwealth, to read the 2026 midterms. Bryce defined healthy democracy as citizens exercising civic responsibility rather than class or partisan interest, and warned that demagogues -- whom he saw in both Conservative leader Benjamin Disraeli and his own Liberal colleagues Gladstone and Lloyd George -- thrive on "indolence, private self-interest, and party spirit" and refashion those vices into democratic virtues. Bryce trusted an educated public opinion, built on universities, public education and a vibrant print culture, to check demagoguery; the essay argues that infrastructure has since decayed, leaving America's 2024 Trump "realignment" reversed just two years later the way Gladstone's Irish Home Rule push ultimately cost his own Liberal Party decades of dominance. It closes pointing to campus "civics centers" in Texas, Florida and Ohio, and to Texas governor-elect Gina Hinojosa's pledge to reinvest in public schools, as rare grounds for optimism.

College football's transformation into a billion-dollar, NIL-and-transfer-portal free market has corroded the sport's character-forming purpose, a new essay argues, and no government agency, university or NCAA fix is likely to put it back together

Law & Liberty's essay, responding to a piece on federal regulation of college sports, argues money has become what sociologist Robert Nisbet called a "cash nexus" that dissolves the loyalty, place-attachment and "sacred" character-forming purpose athletics once carried, turning players into what Nisbet termed "loose individuals" free to transfer for the highest bidder. It cites the University of Michigan paying $7.14 million to its athletic director not to work there, $10 million to its fired offensive coordinator, a likely $25 million buyout to keep head coach Kyle Whittingham, and a possible $17.2 million buyout for an AD who never occupied the chair, alongside the O'Bannon case that opened the door to paying players for name and likeness. Drawing on Johan Huizinga's Homo Ludens, the essay argues sport has become "profane" once play lost its ritual, noncommercial character, and closes warning that online sports gambling -- where "covering the spread" now matters more than winning -- is deepening the rot with no fix in sight.

A new book drawn from Pope Francis' private diaries reveals a man consumed by fear of what he called his own "temptation to omnipotence" and a conviction that "mercy is my real story," a Dominican theologian writes in a review that reconsiders a pontificate he often struggled to defend

First Things' review of Cardinal Víctor Fernández's "The Private Diary of Pope Francis" is written by a Dominican priest and theologian who says he frequently defended Francis's statements to fellow Catholics while privately wishing the pope hadn't made the defense so difficult. The diary entries, kept intermittently from 1992 to August 2024, show Francis returning repeatedly to Ezekiel 16 on divine mercy toward an unfaithful Jerusalem, and naming his episcopal motto miserando atque eligendo as an expression of "mercy is my real story." The most revealing passages concern what Francis called his life's great spiritual temptation -- "the temptation to omnipotence," manifesting as an inability to say no, distrust of others and a drift toward manipulating people -- including an acknowledged period of intense spiritual trial in 2021-2022. The reviewer, who has held senior posts in his own order, closes by applying to Francis the same principle Francis preached: that a person should not be reduced to his failures.
