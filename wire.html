A drone that flaps like a bird slipped past rival detection gear at an Air Force test

The Defense Contract Management Agency said Ornadyne, a U.S. drone maker, flew its O1 ornithopter at the "Game of Drones" event at Edwards Air Force Base, California, Sept. 14-18. The O1 stays airborne on flapping, semi-rigid wings instead of propellers. Chief executive Geourg Kivijian said sensors brought by other companies at the event could not pick the drone out, and that it registered as a bird. Chief technology officer Armen Arakelyan said it was the first time the company could test that against detection systems run by others, and that the flapping wings also make it quieter. Kivijian said his company is the only U.S. manufacturer building mechanical birds for reconnaissance. DCMA's Blue List team ran the event with the Air Force 412th Test Wing's Experimental Test Force and the Griffiss Institute, to put vendors and warfighters in one test range and see how drone capabilities fare against each other.

Pentagon officials asked Baltic allies to tone down public talk about Russia, sources say

Defense News, carrying a Reuters report Friday: four people familiar with the matter said Pentagon officials asked several NATO countries in recent weeks, including Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia, to keep public statements about the Russian threat in check. They said Pentagon policy chief Elbridge Colby was one of the officials delivering the message, and that he cited Europeans saying Russia was "cornered like a rat" as unhelpful. The people did not describe the talks as confrontational. A Pentagon official said private conversations among allies would not be commented on. The report ties the requests to a renewed U.S. push for a negotiated end to the war in Ukraine. The Baltic defense ministries did not respond, and NATO declined to comment.

TruthScript says modern horror runs the Christian story in reverse

TruthScript, Saturday: the essay picks up a debate set off when Blaze commentator Allie Beth Stuckey said Christians should not watch horror films, and Catholic commentator John Doyle answered on his show. It says early films like Dracula, The Mummy, and The Wolf Man let good beat evil, often under Christian symbols. From the late 1960s on, it argues, the genre turned darker, and the larger problem is not the violence but the absence of God. Some films, it says, cast religious authority itself as the menace. Its conclusion: if the Christian story is sin, redemption, and restoration, modern horror tells innocence, sin, and then the destruction of man.

Robert George says Buckley argued with reasons, not strawmen

Law & Liberty, Friday: in a forum reply on academic freedom, Robert P. George answers historian James Hankins, who called William F. Buckley a liberal gentleman. George says the label fits only in the sense of liberal education: teaching the texts and ideas of Western and American civilization without indoctrination and trusting truth to win when views are fairly presented. He notes Hankins left Harvard after 41 years for the Hamilton School at the University of Florida. On Firing Line, George writes, Buckley invited even Marxists to make their best case and answered with reasons, arguments, and evidence.

American Reformer reviews Michael Anton's book on California's late republic

American Reformer, Friday: the review of Michael Anton's Dispatches from the Late Republic opens on California as a state built by migration. It says domestic migration turned negative in the 1990s, immigration from abroad kept the state growing, and around 2017 net migration turned negative for the first time.
