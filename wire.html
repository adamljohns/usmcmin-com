The Joint Armed Forces Color Guard presented the colors Sunday as the National Veterans Parade marched down Constitution Avenue in Washington

A DVIDS photo release Sunday showed members of the Joint Armed Forces Color Guard presenting the colors during the National Veterans Parade along the National Mall in Washington, D.C., Nov. 8. The annual parade brought together service members, veterans organizations, and civilians to honor the service and sacrifice of America's veterans, marching the National Parade Route on Constitution Avenue from the National Archives to the White House. The National Veterans Parade Foundation, which runs the event on corporate and private donations rather than federal funding, opened the weekend Saturday with a high school marching band festival on the Mall between the Washington Monument and the WWII Memorial. The National Weather Service forecast confirms Veterans Day itself, Wednesday, Nov. 11, will be sunny in the capital region with a high near 69.

A Heritage Foundation scorecard says last week's midterm results make codifying Trump-era border policy into law a tougher climb

Heritage Foundation, Thursday: the commentary argues that codifying President Trump's immigration and border policies into statute was never easy with a narrow Republican majority in both chambers, and after Tuesday's midterm results it may be a practical impossibility for the remainder of the term. The piece notes the last major immigration reform law passed in 1996, underscoring how rarely Congress has moved comprehensive legislation on the issue, and argues border and interior enforcement gains made administratively remain exposed to reversal without a statutory anchor.

Israel's president handed Gadi Eisenkot the mandate to form a government after he gathered 67 Knesset recommendations to Netanyahu's 46

WORLD, Sunday night: President Isaac Herzog tasked Yashar! party leader Gadi Eisenkot with building a government after consultations showed Eisenkot held the recommendations of 67 newly elected Knesset members against 46 for incumbent Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, even though Netanyahu's Likud won the popular vote and 27 seats to Yashar!'s 21. Eisenkot asked prospective coalition partners, including Arab Israeli parties, to accept four principles: Israel as a Jewish and democratic state, the values of the Declaration of Independence, mandatory military or national service for all, and recognition of Hamas as a terrorist organization to be destroyed. By law he has 28 days to form a government; if he fails, Herzog may hand the task to another member, possibly Netanyahu, or declare no government possible and trigger a new 90-day election cycle. Netanyahu had not formally responded as of Sunday evening.

III Marine Expeditionary Force extended a junior-ranks curfew on Okinawa after a murder indictment against a U.S. service member

USNI News, Friday: the curfew for grades E-1 through E-4, originally set to expire Sunday, was extended 30 days along with continuing restrictions on alcohol purchase and consumption, restricting those junior Marines and sailors to their personal housing or barracks during curfew hours. The extension follows a murder indictment against a U.S. service member on Okinawa and reflects the command's standard response to incidents that strain the relationship between American forces and host communities on the island.

A Providence essay says Washington has far less leverage freeing Americans jailed abroad for real crimes than it does for political prisoners

Providence, published Monday: the essay distinguishes two classes of Americans held in foreign prisons — those convicted of acts that would also be crimes at home, where the U.S. has little leverage and host governments like Beijing have more to gain from continued detention than Washington has to gain from release, and political prisoners or prisoners of conscience, whose wrongful detention gives the U.S. stronger diplomatic grounds to demand release. The piece points to a Sept. 22 congressional hearing, held two days before Xi Jinping's recent Washington summit, that put both categories of case before the same panel and pressed the administration to treat them as one appeal regardless of the distinction.

Tech / AI

The Space Force is standing up a new mission operations center to knit together the satellite network behind the Golden Dome missile-defense architecture

DefenseScoop, Friday: Space Systems Command announced it awarded Viasat a competitive IDIQ contract worth up to $450 million for network and mission-system integration on the Space Data Network Backbone program, with an initial $115 million task order to design, field, and scale the mission operations center. The center is meant to bring mission integration, network operations, and data-transport management together across a growing multi-vendor, multi-orbit constellation that mixes military-owned and commercial satellites, giving the joint force high-speed, resilient connectivity across air, land, sea, and space domains as the Golden Dome program moves forward.

Culture

A Law & Liberty essay says America's most radical protesters still demand the strict legal courtesies of an offended aristocrat

Law & Liberty, Monday: the essay observes that the most extreme American protesters pair seemingly lawless street behavior — shouting, shoving, calls to tear down the Constitution — with a fastidious insistence on their own legal protections, citing a recent No Kings protest where a demonstrator who had been part of confrontational street theater nonetheless warned a conservative interviewer, 'don't touch me.' The piece traces this contradiction to a long American tradition of litigiousness documented back to colonial Virginia and nineteenth-century St. Louis, arguing the instinct to combine revolutionary rhetoric with bourgeois legal propriety runs across the political spectrum, not just one side.

An American Reformer essay asks whether any hymn, church, or book made today is worth handing down to Christians a century from now

American Reformer, published Nov. 2: the essay argues cultural Christianity is making a comeback, and with it comes the harder work of building a culture actually worth passing on — hymns, church architecture, art, furniture, and books of sufficient quality that great-great-grandchildren would want to keep them. The author contends little produced in the current era clears that bar, and frames the renewed interest in Christian culture as an opportunity, not a victory already won, to build from the ground up rather than simply reclaim ground lost.
