The Pentagon committed $450 million to Neros Technologies to help rebuild America's small-drone industrial base

Defense News, Monday: the Department of Defense's Office of Strategic Capital signed a $450 million conditional loan with California-based Neros Technologies to expand domestic manufacturing of small drone components, with Neros committing another $165 million of its own money to bring the total investment to $615 million. Neros' head of growth, Ross Pederson, told Military Times the expansion is "a huge benefit to the American taxpayer," noting that roughly 99% of small drone components are currently made in China. Office of Strategic Capital director David Lorch said Neros had already demonstrated an ability to design, manufacture and deliver small unmanned aircraft systems. Neros and drone maker PDW, which received an $820 million OSC loan in July, are both competing in the Pentagon's Drone Dominance Program for small combat drones.

The Royal Navy commissioned its first unmanned surface vessel squadron last week as it builds toward a hybrid crewed-uncrewed fleet

USNI News, Monday: the Royal Navy's 1st Autonomous Squadron was formally commissioned in a ceremony Thursday at His Majesty's Naval Base Portsmouth, marking the service's first dedicated unmanned surface vessel unit. The squadron is part of a broader British push to field a hybrid fleet pairing traditional crewed warships with autonomous vessels for tasks like mine-hunting and surveillance, part of a wider allied shift toward unmanned maritime systems.

Young left-leaning voters cite economic grievances, not just identity causes, as what draws them to socialism

Public Discourse, Monday: the author reports that young people who lean toward the new left often cite serious economic concerns, not only the identitarian causes like climate change and immigration commonly assumed to be the draw. The piece argues that engaging those economic grievances directly, rather than dismissing Gen Z's turn toward socialism as purely cultural, is the harder and more necessary response.

A defense firm is converting standard fishing trawlers into autonomous submarine-hunters for NATO's Arctic flank

Defense News, Monday: the company's "Arctic Hunter" concept starts with an industry-standard 56-meter trawler hull, strips out the fish-processing equipment, and replaces it with autonomy and sensor systems meant to track Russian submarine activity in northern waters. The approach is built around fielding sea-hardened, affordable platforms quickly by reusing a hull design the fishing industry already builds and maintains at scale.

Tech / AI

Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation into CEO Jensen Huang over reports he was targeted by a foreign intelligence effort

WORLD, Monday: the chipmaker's board said Sunday it is examining allegations that Huang, 63, was targeted by a foreign intelligence effort, and that it has no reason to believe national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised. Huang will keep running the company without interruption. In a memo to employees, Huang said he had been unfaithful to his wife, said there was no excuse for it, and pledged full cooperation with the probe. The New York Times and Financial Times had reported an affair with a Chinese businesswoman they identified as gathering information for Beijing. China's Foreign Ministry called the spy claims an unsubstantiated smear. President Trump called Huang an honorable guy and said he would support bringing him on his upcoming Asia trip. Before the board acted, House Republicans on the China select committee had requested a briefing from Huang, citing concern over Chinese access to cutting-edge U.S. technology.

Nineteenth-century Ireland paired sectarian conflict with a serious Catholic and Protestant debate over representative government

Law & Liberty, Monday: the review examines a new book on Ireland after Edmund Burke, arguing that nineteenth-century Irish politics cannot be reduced to simple sectarian strife. Catholic and Protestant thinkers alike engaged in serious argument about what representative government should look like in a divided society, a debate with echoes in today's arguments over pluralism and self-rule.

First Things, Monday: reviewing Randy Boyagoda's new novel "Lords of Serendipity," the essay asks whether the campus novel still works as a genre when the university itself, as an institution commanding cultural authority, is in decline.
