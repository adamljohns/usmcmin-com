Defense News

Hackers have breached the Air Force's flagship Cloud One computing platform, a hub that powers personnel, pay, logistics and air-defense data feeds across the service, with Cyber Command now called in to assess the damage

Defense News, citing Reuters sourcing, reports the intrusion at Cloud One was discovered within the past month during an upgrade effort, and has since caused a range of disruption: migration of back-office applications to the platform has been paused, software updates already running on Cloud One were blocked, and some users had administrator rights revoked. Cloud One, launched in 2017 and combining Microsoft, Amazon, Oracle and Alphabet cloud resources, powers the Air Force's Cloud-Based Command and Control system, which collates air-defense radar feeds across North America, and the Theater Integrated Combat Munitions System, which tracks bombs and missiles at more than 200 Air Force locations worldwide. One source likened the compromised control plane to a universal remote, saying intruders could in theory check the operational status of aircraft or spare-parts movement, though it is unclear whether they did. The Air Force said it does not comment on the security posture of specific networks; Cyber Command did not respond to requests for comment. The breach is the latest in a string of recently disclosed intrusions across federal agencies, including two at the FBI and one at the Treasury Department.

Political

A nostalgic retelling of Ottoman history is giving political cover to Turkish leaders who talk openly of one day "liberating" Jerusalem, a new essay argues, even as the actual historical record includes pogroms, forced expulsions and the Armenian genocide

Providence traces how Turkish officials, including the interior minister and a close ally of President Erdogan, have invoked a narrative of Ottoman-era religious tolerance to justify neo-Ottoman ambitions toward Syria and Jerusalem, a narrative amplified by U.S. Ambassador to Turkey Tom Barrack's praise of the old Ottoman millet system. The essay argues that history complicates the nostalgia: Jews and Christians lived as second-class dhimmis under the empire, a sectarian civil war killed more than 15,000 Christians in Damascus and Mount Lebanon in 1860, the Ottoman government carried out the Armenian genocide beginning in 1915, and Turkey's wartime government later refused to admit the Jewish refugee ship Struma in 1942, after which it was sunk with the loss of 768 lives. The essay warns the real danger isn't a Turkish march on Jerusalem but Washington accepting the friendly version of this history and backing Turkish regional ambitions while dismissing Israeli concerns.

Providence

Politics cannot supply the meaning Americans are looking for after a bruising midterm, a new essay argues — only the family can, and both parties have let a "vice economy" of gambling, marijuana and pornography fill the gap instead

The Institute for Family Studies president writes that government's deepening entanglement with big business has fed corruption and overheated political rhetoric, and argues the health of the American family — not any single demographic group — should be the measuring stick for public policy. He faults Republicans for ceding ground on a "vice economy" of sports gambling, marijuana and pornography that research links to intimate-partner violence and financial strain, and for categorically rejecting AI safeguards even as Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang lobbied to keep chip sales open to China while reportedly involved with an alleged Chinese spy. The essay calls for reorienting policy — on housing affordability, child tax credits and AI regulation — around family formation rather than entitlement growth, citing IFS research that homeownership among Americans under 35 has fallen from 50% (1960-1980) to 30% today.

Culture

Grand Theft Auto 6 is a technical triumph thirteen years in the making, a new essay concedes, but argues Christians — and everyone else — should skip it because the game catechizes players into vice regardless of how beautiful it looks

TruthScript's review calls GTA 6 near-photorealistic and immersive but argues beauty is not virtue, citing Ephesians 5's call to make the best use of time because "the days are evil." The essay says the franchise has evolved from a cartoonish 1990s crime game into a world saturated with drugs, prostitution and casual violence, and argues the real draw for men under 40 — many reportedly skipping work for the release — is less the content than the scarcity of remaining male-majority spaces after decades of institutions being, in the essay's words, opened and culturally reoriented. It closes with a call for men to build real communities, businesses and churches rather than invest hours in the game.

TruthScript

America remains a single nation despite its "crazy quilt of differences," a new essay argues, but warns the country's own Cold War-era ambition to become a borderless empire of rights and commerce is the likeliest path to losing that nationhood this century

Writing in First Things, the author — a contributor to the new book "Five Views on Christian Nationalism" — argues foreign visitors still recognize a distinct American character, pointing to the global reach of American media and the way children of immigrants in Texas pick up regional identity within a generation. He argues the deeper threat to American nationhood isn't mass migration itself but two post-Cold War projects: a liberal "Empire of Rights" built on universal human-rights theory that treats national loyalty as parochial, and an "Empire of Commerce" built on free trade and globalized capital that treats national interest as inefficient. Both, he argues, are quietly pulling the United States toward the kind of post-national, administrative future already visible in the European Union.
