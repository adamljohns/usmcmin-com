Feeding 5,000 Sailors a Thanksgiving meal aboard a moving aircraft carrier takes months of planning, and this year the crew of USS George Washington pulled it off in the Indian Ocean after a mid-deployment fleet transition briefly complicated their supply chain

DVIDS reports holiday food shipment planning for George Washington's Thanksgiving meal began as early as July, with the first loadout departing the continental U.S. in early fall and the final critical items arriving just before the holiday. A transition between fleet areas of operations created a temporary obstacle for the ship's supply chain, resolved through the supply department's planning, according to supply officer Cmdr. Adam Hilliard, who called it "a no-fail mission." Culinary specialists split into specialized turkey, sides and bakery teams, firing ovens at 2 a.m. Thanksgiving morning and keeping them running until 3:30 p.m., while offering a modified breakfast and lunch menu to free up equipment for the feast. "Giving thanks is not just a tradition, but a reminder of the resilience and unity that makes our crew strong," said Chief Warrant Officer 3 Krystle Mattia, the ship's food services officer. "We provide a service to Sailors all across the fleet that cannot go home," said Chief Culinary Specialist Raymon Solomon. "I think it's a beautiful thing."

Politics cannot furnish Americans with meaning, but it can create the conditions for families and churches to flourish, a new Public Discourse essay argues, calling the American family the one institution capable of uniting the warring factions of post-midterm politics

The essay, by the head of the Institute for Family Studies, agrees with former Hungarian premier Viktor Orban that politics cannot furnish meaning, arguing both campaign-fundraising incentives and government's expanding entanglement with big business push politics toward false, existential stakes and growing corruption — corruption the author says contributed heavily to the recent midterm's "blue wave." The piece outlines five fronts for a family-centered politics: curbing the "vice economy" of gambling ads, marijuana and pornography that research links to intimate-partner violence and bankruptcy; guiding rather than banning artificial intelligence, after polling found voters of all stripes rejected federal preemption of state AI child-safety laws; talking about marriage in terms legible to the political left as well as right; modest family-policy wins like the One Big Beautiful Bill's Child Tax Credit expansion, measured against the far larger entitlements seniors receive; and cutting the land-use regulation the author says has driven homeownership among Americans under 35 from 50 percent in 1960-1980 to just 30 percent today.

The Pentagon still lacks a single office or department-wide plan to fix persistent munitions shortages that have been strained further by support for the wars in Ukraine and Iran, a new unclassified Government Accountability Office report finds

USNI News carried the GAO's Nov. 19 report, "DOD Munitions: Planning and Leadership Needed to Address Persistent Shortages," submitted to the House Armed Services Committee, which cites two unfilled needs: a high-level office empowered to develop, manage and execute a comprehensive, department-wide munitions program, and centralized, consistent oversight of munitions data. The report follows a classified version two months earlier that GAO said exposed "trends in DOD munitions inventories, persistent shortfalls, and how these shortfalls have affected DOD's support to war plans," even as the Pentagon has pursued foreign production deals and fielded new systems like laser-guided rockets to rebuild stockpiles. news.usni.org 403'd (Cloudflare challenge) on same-turn fetch; this item's added detail comes from a same-turn clean (200) fetch of defense-trade coverage quoting the GAO report directly, not invented.

Connecticut Governor Jonathan Trumbull's 1782 Thanksgiving proclamation was the first to set the last Thursday in November as the holiday's official date, an American Reformer post notes, a tradition that followed hundreds of similar British colonial-era announcements before it

American Reformer's post on Trumbull's Oct. 31, 1782 proclamation — issued after the Revolutionary War — notes its contents were hardly unique, since hundreds of similar thanksgiving announcements had already been made by British colonial authorities and American governors before it; what made Trumbull's distinct, per the post, was fixing the last Thursday in November as the official annual date. The piece stops behind a subscriber paywall after its opening paragraph; the insight stays within that paragraph.

An English-born writer who emigrated to the U.S. in his mid-thirties says adopting Thanksgiving taught him something his homeland's own civic feasts never did: true gratitude, he argues, is the thing that makes us most human, and it is in short supply in a social-media age that rewards outrage instead

First Things' essay contrasts the writer's boyhood memories of England's Guy Fawkes Night — burning the "Catholic traitor" in effigy every Nov. 5 — with the Thanksgiving tradition he has adopted in his American home, built around gathering his small immigrant family with gratitude "for each other and gratitude to God." He argues gratitude trades at a discount in the modern world, as the loss of ordinary "please and thank you" courtesies gives way to social media, which he says rewards outrage, anger, bitterness and self-promotion rather than genuine thanks, even turning expressions of gratitude into subtle self-promotion. True thankfulness, he writes, is what makes us "truly human."

The Museum of the Bible's newly opened Bowden Gallery of Biblical Art has mounted its first exhibit, 22 artifacts spanning more than 500 years that depict the Annunciation, the moment the angel Gabriel told Mary she would conceive the Son of God, a new WORLD feature says

WORLD reports the exhibit, titled The Annunciation: Picturing the Incarnation of Christ, ranges from an illustration in the 1493 Nuremberg Chronicle smaller than a postcard to Bruce Herman's 2006 gold-leafed panel Overshadowed, a full-length portrait of Mary staring up in wonder. None of the 22 pieces were created as icons for veneration of Mary; curator Amy Van Dyke describes one Delft tile from the Calvinist Netherlands, likely made for a fireplace, as having "brought religious imagery into the home where it was OK to do because it was a functional thing." The exhibit's main attraction is not a framed work but a life-sized scene.
