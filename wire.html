Airmen priced simulated port repairs at Naha during Beverly Sword 27-1

The Air Force said Airmen from the 18th Contracting Squadron and 18th Comptroller Squadron worked a scenario on Oct. 30 in which an attack damaged bollards and rubber fenders at Naha Military Port, Japan. Civil engineers from the 718th Civil Engineer Squadron estimated repair timelines. Contracting negotiated prices with simulated contractors. Finance verified the paperwork and processed payments. Senior Airman Brittany Walsch, a financial operations technician, said the mission cannot continue without funding. Senior Airman Neil Calica, a contracting specialist, said the first job was getting boats back into the port, then clearing debris, because each step depended on the one before it. The team used $25,000 in Japanese yen as play money to test payments in the field.

Pentagon officials asked Baltic governments to quiet their public talk about Russia

Defense News reported Friday that Pentagon officials have asked Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia in recent weeks to tone down public statements about the threat from Russia. Four people familiar with the matter, speaking anonymously, said the messages went out in bilateral meetings. A Pentagon official said private conversations among allies would not be discussed. The people said policy chief Elbridge Colby was one of the officials delivering the message, and that he told a Baltic official there should be no poking directed at the Russians. One person said Colby pointed to talk of Russia being cornered like a rat. Estonian Prime Minister Kristen Michal used a similar phrase in late September. The defense ministries of the three Baltic states did not respond. NATO declined to comment.

Defense News

Palau and U.S. leaders met in Honolulu and restated the Compact of Free Association

Indo-Pacific Command said senior leaders from Palau and the United States met Oct. 28 at the Hale Koa Hotel in Honolulu for a joint committee meeting. Palau President Surangel S. Whipps Jr., National Security Coordinator Jennifer Anson, and officials from Indo-Pacific Command, Joint Task Force-Micronesia, and the U.S. Embassy sat in. Whipps called the Compact of Free Association a living bond rooted in mutual sacrifice and shared democratic values. Stephen Dreikorn, deputy chief of mission at the embassy in Koror, said the joint objective is collective security and measurable benefits for both countries. Palau gained independence in 1981 and entered the compact in 1994. The islands sit in the Western Pacific, bordering the Philippines.

Destroyer Spruance has been deployed 342 days, past the Navy's usual seven-month mark

Defense News reported Thursday that the Arleigh Burke-class destroyer USS Spruance left Naval Base San Diego on Nov. 21, 2025, with the Abraham Lincoln carrier strike group and stayed out after the carrier came home. As of Oct. 29 the ship had been deployed 342 days. Marie Bailey, whose husband serves aboard, told Military Times that families have been told the ship would return next month for several months running. Two sailors, granted anonymity, said life on a destroyer is as hard as life on a bigger ship and that the crew's families matter as much as a carrier's. Defense News also reported that John Finn, Mustin, and Rafael Peralta have had expected returns pushed from 2026 into 2027. The Navy has not published a full count of extended destroyer deployments.

Tech / AI

Army cyber leaders spent two days on a threat they called superintelligence

Army Cyber Command said the Office of the Chief Information Officer and Army Cyber Command hosted a virtual cybersecurity forum Oct. 28 and 29. The theme was modernizing Army cybersecurity for a superintelligence-enabled threat landscape. Lt. Gen. Christopher L. Eubank, who commands Army Cyber Command, said awareness month cannot be a box check, and that when the network is compromised, lethality is compromised. Acting chief information officer Gabe Chiulli said superintelligence is advancing at an unprecedented pace and that cybersecurity will have to take a different approach than it has in the past. The forum was aimed at the Army's cyber posture, its workforce, and incident response.

Law & Liberty says Sleepy Hollow is how America learned to live with its dead

A Friday Law & Liberty essay says Washington Irving, not a later novelist, founded American literature's taste for ghosts. It treats The Legend of Sleepy Hollow, published in 1820 and set in 1790, as more than a Halloween chase. The Hudson River Valley in the story was the ground of some of the bloodiest fighting in the Revolution, including neighbor against neighbor. The essay says Ichabod Crane's flight from the Headless Horseman was Irving's way of asking how the dead of that war still shape what Americans see. It quotes Federalist Fisher Ames, who in 1804 doubted the young republic would ever match Homer or Plato, and answers that Irving found the American landscape already haunted.

Robert George says Buckley trusted truth more than a thumb on the scale

Law & Liberty published Friday a reply by Robert P. George to a forum on his essay about God, man, and academic freedom. George says William F. Buckley would have smiled at being called a liberal gentleman, because Buckley built the modern conservative movement to oppose what American politics called liberalism. The liberalism George means is liberal education: the ideas and texts of Western and American civilization, presented so truth can persuade, not a catechism. James Hankins left Harvard after 41 years for the Hamilton School at the University of Florida. George says Buckley still wanted professors to favor Christianity and individualism, and that the reconciliation is the man himself, who argued hard and still treated opponents as worth answering.

Providence says cheap autonomous weapons can make a long war easier to keep

A Friday Providence essay ties Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's announcement of a combatant command for autonomous warfare to battlefields already using drones in Ukraine and the Strait of Hormuz. The writer says mass-produced, inexpensive systems let a smaller country hold off a larger one, and that the ethical question is no longer only the trigger. If drones cut casualties, both sides can keep fighting on a footing their publics will accept, and ceasefire talks can harden. The essay notes Ukraine's claim this year to have taken and held ground with autonomous systems, and says Russia could answer in kind. It treats the American use of the same tools to search for downed pilots as a different moral case: a promise to bring people home.
