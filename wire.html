Marines, Okinawa police and Japanese community volunteers teamed up this week for joint foot patrols near U.S. installations on Okinawa, keeping curfew and liberty rules on track during a sensitive stretch for the American base presence there

A U.S. Marine Corps photographer documented the Nov. 19 patrols, which paired Okinawa Prefectural Police officers and city volunteers with U.S. service members walking neighborhoods near the installations. The joint patrols are a recurring piece of III Marine Expeditionary Force's effort to promote public safety and keep Marines and sailors compliant with enhanced liberty policies, curfew rules and disciplinary standards — a visible, hands-on complement to the joint training and coastal-radar work the Corps has also been doing with Philippine forces elsewhere in the Pacific this month.

Political

President Trump's newly concluded Greenland security deal with Denmark and Greenland's governments gives Washington lasting Arctic access while leaving Danish and Greenlandic sovereignty intact, a new policy analysis finds, pushing back on the Arctic land-grab framing critics raised when Trump first floated acquiring the island

The commentary argues the agreement gives the United States the security access it needs in the Arctic while leaving Danish and Greenlandic sovereignty over the island intact, calling it a model for how great-power competition with Russia and China over Arctic resources and shipping routes can be managed without the territorial coercion critics feared when Trump first raised the idea of acquiring Greenland. It frames the deal as a concrete security gain that should outlast the current administration.

Heritage Foundation

America has quietly become a low-trust society — visible in everything from locked-up store shelves to a landmark survey in which, for the first time, most Americans say their fellow citizens are not morally good

The essay traces General Social Survey data showing the share of Americans who say 'most people can be trusted' has fallen from just under half in 1972 to about one-quarter by 2024, and cites a 2026 Pew survey of 25 nations in which the U.S. was the only country where a majority of respondents said most of their fellow citizens are morally bad. It argues that collapsing trust forces society to replace free, informal cooperation with expensive rules and enforcement — the plexiglass cases around store goods being one visible symptom — and warns that democracy itself depends on a baseline of trust in neighbors, institutions and the ballot count that America can no longer take for granted.

Tech / AI

Two German one-way attack drones passed live-fire trials this month, clearing the way for the German army to start fielding loitering munitions and training troops on them before the end of the year

Germany's military procurement office said Stark's Virtus and Helsing's HX-2 both reliably hit designated targets and detonated as intended during trials the week of Nov. 9 at the Bundeswehr's Meppen weapons test center, though the office did not disclose how many shots were fired or the systems' success rates. The tests follow a February vote by German lawmakers approving contracts worth roughly $294 million apiece for the two firms, with options that could add $1 billion more per deal; the German army's 45th Armored Brigade in Lithuania is slated to be the first major unit equipped starting in 2027, part of Berlin's broader post-2022 shift toward arming itself with attack drones.

Defense News

The future aircraft carrier CVN-81 will keep the name USS Doris Miller, honoring the enlisted Pearl Harbor hero, as HII's Newport News Shipbuilding prepares to lay the ship's ceremonial keel on Dec. 5

USNI News reported the Navy has settled the ship's name after a monthslong renaming review, confirming Doris Miller — a Black mess attendant who manned an anti-aircraft gun during the Dec. 7, 1941 attack on Pearl Harbor and was later awarded the Navy Cross — will remain the carrier's namesake. The decision closes out uncertainty that had surrounded the ship's name since the Pentagon's broader review of vessel names tied to diversity-era naming decisions, and keeps intact the Navy's recognition of Miller as the first Black American to receive the Navy Cross.

Culture

The explosion of sports-betting apps and prediction markets like Kalshi has moved gambling from society's seedy margins into the mainstream of American life, and Christians who wave it off as harmless entertainment are missing the sin underneath it

The piece traces gambling's fall from a near-universal American taboo enforced by law in every state through Nevada's 1931 legalization and the Supreme Court's 2018 ruling striking down the federal sports-betting ban, arguing the habit has since gone mainstream through apps like FanDuel, DraftKings and Kalshi that let users 'trade' on sports, weather, elections and even military conflicts. It argues gambling violates the Bible's ban on covetousness because it is a zero-sum transfer of wealth from a loser to a winner rather than a righteous exchange of labor, goods or risk capital, and warns that churches normalizing poker nights and fantasy-football wagers are discipling a generation — including the author's own college-athlete son — into an industry where, as he puts it, the house always wins.

TruthScript

Secretary of State Marco Rubio's Acropolis speech frames the West's real crisis as not power but a loss of spiritedness — the ancient Greek thumos — replaced by a therapeutic culture more worried about shielding people from failure than celebrating excellence

Rubio delivered the October speech on the Acropolis, telling his audience the West has traded the ancient Greek pursuit of honor and risk for 'a world of perfect safety, devoid of risk or failure,' and that the 2020 wave of monument removal and historical self-condemnation taught a generation of Americans shame rather than pride in their inheritance. The essay, by First Things editor R.R. Reno, reads Rubio's call for 'the unapologetic pursuit of national power' as an attempt to revive thumos — spiritedness — arguing that recovering a capacity for collective pride and ambition, not just stronger supply chains or defense budgets, is what will determine whether the West actually has a future.

First Things

A new gallery at the Museum of the Bible in Washington gathers 22 artworks spanning five centuries — from a tiny 1493 woodcut to pieces by Andy Warhol and Salvador Dalí — all depicting the moment the angel Gabriel told Mary she would bear the Son of God

WORLD's report on the newly opened Bowden Gallery of Biblical Art says the exhibit, called 'The Annunciation: Picturing the Incarnation of Christ,' deliberately avoids icons meant for venerating Mary and instead collects illustrations and artistic depictions of the moment, including a Delft tile from the Calvinist Netherlands that once adorned a fireplace and artist Theodore Prescott's life-sized installation of a startled, plaster-cast Mary lit by a neon outline of the angel. Curator Amy Van Dyke told WORLD the range of artists, including famously secular ones like Warhol and Dalí, shows how deeply the Annunciation has threaded through Western art regardless of an artist's own faith; the exhibit runs through early April 2027, timed to close near the Feast of the Annunciation.
