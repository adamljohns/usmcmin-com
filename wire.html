Two Navy F-35C stealth fighters completed the jet's first launch and recovery trials aboard a Ford-class aircraft carrier, clearing a path for the Navy's newest carriers to fly the fifth-generation jet

Over 11 days of testing that began Oct. 13, two F-35C Lightning IIs from the "Salty Dogs" of Air Test and Evaluation Squadron 23 made 34 flights and logged 122 catapult launches and 122 arrested landings aboard the future USS John F. Kennedy, the Navy's second Ford-class carrier, Lockheed Martin said. Unlike Nimitz-class carriers, which use steam catapults and hydraulic arresting gear, Ford-class ships rely on the Electromagnetic Aircraft Launch System and Advanced Arresting Gear, and Kennedy's island sits about 140 feet farther back on a redesigned flight deck. "This is a critical event for the F-35C, Kennedy, and the United States Navy," said Sean Jackson, Lockheed's F-35 Development Program vice president, adding that the Navy has now cleared the jet for operations across its entire carrier fleet pending launch and recovery bulletins. Kennedy, built with the modifications already installed, took preliminary acceptance in September and is due for formal delivery in spring 2027.

The 1801 'midnight judges' episode, when a lame-duck Federalist Congress created a wave of new federal judgeships in the final weeks before Thomas Jefferson's inauguration, is drawing fresh attention as a precedent on lame-duck government power

The commentary recalls that in the winter of 1801, a defeated Federalist majority, with only weeks left before Jefferson's inauguration, created a batch of new federal judgeships, and that President John Adams spent his final hours in office signing the commissions — the episode historically known as the "midnight judges."

Heritage Foundation

States that force political parties to open their primaries to non-members violate the First Amendment right of association, a Law & Liberty essay argues, closing out a three-part defense of closed primaries

The essay argues mandatory open and semi-open primary laws violate parties' First Amendment right to exclude non-members, pointing to the Supreme Court's rulings in Democratic Party v. Wisconsin ex rel. LaFollette (1981) and California Democratic Party v. Jones (2000), which protected party governance from state-mandated open primaries. It answers a recent defense of open-primary laws point by point: the right of association, it argues, belongs to parties as well as individuals, since groups like newspapers routinely exclude outsiders from their internal work; state funding of primaries doesn't entitle the state to override that right, since states fund primaries for their own administrative reasons rather than as a favor to parties; and Smith v. Allwright and Terry v. Adams limited state election rules under the Fifteenth Amendment rather than establishing that party primaries are free of First Amendment protection. The piece notes pending open-primary challenges in Texas and Colorado and says a Supreme Court ruling against mandatory open primaries remains a real possibility if either case reaches the justices.

Law & Liberty

Vice President JD Vance is right that the Iran war hurt Republicans at the ballot box but wrong that the war itself was a mistake, a Providence commentary argues, faulting President Trump's execution rather than the decision to fight

The commentary says letting Iran rebuild its military and nuclear program would cost far more later than finishing the fight now, and faults Trump for failing to make the case for the war as a vital national interest, for repeatedly overpromising how quickly it would end, and for calling off military operations before destroying Iran's missile capability and extracting its enriched uranium. It criticizes a June memorandum of understanding Vance negotiated with Iran as echoing Iranian talking points, and warns that having Vance and Middle East envoy Steve Witkoff lead negotiations risks a deal that leaves the regime in place without real constraints on its nuclear program.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced a new US-Venezuela military partnership on his first visit to Caracas since American forces helped remove President Nicolas Maduro in a January raid

Hegseth announced the agreement alongside Venezuelan acting President Delcy Rodriguez, covering joint training, intelligence sharing and other military cooperation, and said it would mean a major ramp-up in joint action against drug-trafficking groups: "To the narco terrorists who have used this as a safe haven, your time is up," he said. The visit comes as Washington's influence in Venezuela's oil sector keeps growing under an August deal giving producer North American Blue Energy Partners long-term access to oilfields holding roughly a fifth of the country's reserves, with the U.S. holding a 35% stake in NABEP's parent company; Hegseth said the arrangement makes the Strait of Hormuz, where Iran has been firing on tankers during the nine-month Iran war, "a non-factor for the Western Hemisphere." He invoked what he called a revamped "Donroe Doctrine" asserting U.S. primacy in the hemisphere, then flew on to Colombia to deliver a C-130 aircraft and discuss security cooperation, where a dispute emerged over whether Bogota had rejected a separate U.S. proposal for joint strikes on Colombian armed groups.

Defense News

The Pentagon has no overarching plan tying munitions production to what the military actually needs, a Government Accountability Office report warns, even as the department rushes to rebuild depleted stockpiles

The GAO study, the public version of a classified report finished in September, found the Defense Department "does not have an overarching plan that links munitions production and funding to requirements," leaving it and the services reactive to global events rather than driven by a total requirement. It says the Army's rush to boost 155mm artillery shell production, strained further by a $500 million Texas plant that has yet to produce shell components, was not driven by any assessment of the Pentagon's total munitions needs. The report also found the Pentagon's own tracking tool, the Munitions Readiness Initiative, pulls from 37 data sources but is not consistently reconciled against the services' own inventory systems, and that authority over munitions stockpiles is fragmented across multiple offices with no single office accountable for fixing it. GAO recommended DoD create an overarching plan and metrics to match production, funding and requirements.

'The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping' is a competent enough prequel, but it recycles the franchise's oppression-and-violence message without adding much to its 25-year-old mythology, a new review finds

The review says the film, the second Hunger Games prequel, fills in the backstory of Haymitch Abernathy's forced entry into the Capitol's games on his 16th birthday, 24 years before the original story, with Joseph Zada taking over the role from Woody Harrelson and Ralph Fiennes playing a younger President Snow. It calls the film a satisfying-enough franchise entry for fans but says it is "rather awkward as a prequel," since the revelation of a 25-year conspiracy against the Capitol doesn't square with Haymitch's despair in the original film, and faults the movie for having tributes form alliances in an arena built to turn captives against each other. The reviewer concludes the fight choreography is "just a step above pitiful" and says the franchise's repeated warnings about oppression and violence are wearing thin after five films.

A self-styled 'New Christian Right' that surfaced at a recent conference is built on anti-Semitism and white nationalism dressed up in Christian language, not a recovery of historic Christian tradition, a new essay argues

The essay examines last week's "Christ Is King: America After Trump" conference, where organizer Joel Webbon called for a "New Christian Right" built on four pillars: anti-Zionism, male leadership, "race realism" and "Christ is King." It argues those terms mean, respectively, anti-Semitism, citing Webbon's calls to "righteously despise Israel"; contempt for women in leadership; white nationalism, citing Webbon's past defense of slavery as a "blessing"; and a citation-heavy appeal to fringe Christian sources to justify the rest. The piece notes speakers included conspiracy theorist Alex Jones and internet commentator Nick Fuentes, and argues the movement's two-tiered theology, which treats "nature" as readable apart from God, echoes a scheme once used to justify Nazi-era political theology and was rejected by theologians Henri de Lubac, Karl Barth and Dietrich Bonhoeffer. It concludes the movement's "Christian nationalism" functions as ethnic identitarianism rather than shared doctrine or church communion, making it, despite the slogans, "a more godless political vision."
