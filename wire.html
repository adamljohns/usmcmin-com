The Navy's nominee for its No. 2 civilian post told senators the service wants a new foreign-owned shipyard built in the United States specifically to produce small surface combatants, following the model already used for the Constellation-class frigate program

Pressed by Sen. Angus King (I-Maine) on whether he supports building U.S. warships in foreign-owned yards, the Navy's nominee for under secretary said yes, telling lawmakers Thursday that the service wants a new shipyard, owned and paid for by a foreign shipbuilder, dedicated to small surface combatants, according to USNI News. The approach mirrors the Navy's existing Constellation-class guided-missile frigate program, whose ships are being built by Fincantieri Marinette Marine — the Wisconsin-based U.S. subsidiary of the Italian shipbuilder Fincantieri — under a contract awarded in 2020. The Constellation class is intended to be the Navy's next-generation small surface combatant, succeeding the Freedom- and Independence-class littoral combat ships.

Political

Neither major party has a coherent foreign-policy strategy heading into the next two years, and Washington should expect sustained political fights rather than passive drift over Ukraine, China and America's role abroad

The analysis argues the next two years will bring contestation rather than drift, with Ukraine as a likely central battlefield as President Trump seeks a negotiated settlement while congressional Republicans and Democrats who regard resisting Russian aggression as essential to European security push back. It says Republicans are split between Trump's transactional, sovereignty-first approach and traditional internationalists committed to NATO and Ukraine, while Democrats split between traditional internationalists, progressives skeptical of intervention and defense spending, and economic nationalists who sometimes converge with Trump on trade. The piece argues world order is increasingly defined by great-power spheres of influence — Russia in Eastern Europe, China in East Asia, and the U.S. reasserting dominance in the Western Hemisphere under a revived Monroe Doctrine — and calls for a "Liberal International Order 2.0" that is more realistic about power while still defending sovereignty, alliances and the rule of law.

Providence

Plexiglass cages over toothpaste and locked cabinets at the mall are symptoms of a decades-long collapse in American interpersonal and institutional trust that is reshaping daily life

The essay cites the General Social Survey, which found that in 1972, just under half of Americans agreed most people can be trusted, a share that fell to barely one-quarter by 2024 — a decline of roughly one point every three years for fifty years. It notes trust has fallen across political institutions, media, financial institutions, corporations, religion, police and schools, with only small businesses and the military still holding public confidence, and cites a 2026 Pew survey of 25 nations in which the United States was the only country where a majority of citizens believe most of their fellow countrymen are morally bad. The essay argues declining trust forces society to replace free trust with costly rules and assurances — plexiglass cages, waivers, compliance bureaucracies — in a feedback loop that breeds more distrust, warning that democracy depends on trust between citizens, government and neighbors.

Law & Liberty

Claims that diverse corporate boards and management teams produce stronger financial results rest on weaker statistical evidence than commonly assumed, invoking a classic warning about mistaking correlation for causation

The commentary, from The Heritage Foundation, opens by recalling Darrell Huff's 1954 book "How to Lie with Statistics," one of the best-selling statistics books in American publishing history, built on the insight that correlation dressed up as causation can mislead even careful readers. Applying that caution to corporate-governance research, the piece argues studies claiming board and management diversity drives better financial results often fail to establish the causal link they claim — a caution relevant to corporate boards, investors and regulators weighing diversity-linked governance proposals.

Tech / AI

The U.S. has burned through stockpiles of key long-range missiles fighting Iran, and may have to fall back on shorter-range weapons in a future fight against a stronger adversary

The CSIS report, using data current as of Nov. 3, found the United States expended between a third and two-thirds of key munition inventories during the 39-day campaign against Iran that ended with an April 8 ceasefire and in subsequent operations. It lists Patriot, THAAD and Standard Missiles as "heavily used, no substitutes," and long-range strike weapons including Tomahawks, JASSM cruise missiles and Precision Strike Missiles as heavily used with only some substitutes, while short-range munitions such as small-diameter bombs and AIM-120 AMRAAMs face no significant constraints. The report concludes the U.S. currently has enough munitions to renew a fight against Iran but says rebuilding inventories for a potential conflict with China "will require a multiyear commitment," warning that production increases are outpaced by demand: "The problem is not money ... but time."

Defense News

The U.S. expanded its naval blockade of Iran to cover any ship that takes on cargo transferred at sea from a vessel that loaded at an Iranian port, as attacks on shipping in the region continue

According to USNI News, the expanded blockade, tracked by the Joint Maritime Information Center, now treats any ship receiving cargo via a ship-to-ship transfer from a vessel that called at an Iranian port or coastline as subject to the same restrictions as ships sailing directly to or from Iran. The move is the latest step in a U.S. naval blockade of Iran that has run alongside continued attacks on shipping near the Strait of Hormuz.

Culture

Sports gambling apps and prediction markets like Kalshi have moved gambling out of the shadows and into the mainstream of American life, and Christians should recognize it as sin rather than harmless entertainment

The essay traces how gambling, once illegal and culturally shunned in America for nearly two centuries, became normalized after Nevada legalized it in 1931 and the Supreme Court struck down a federal sports-betting ban in 2018, and notes platforms like Kalshi now let users "trade" on sports, weather, elections and even military conflicts. It argues gambling violates biblical economic principles because it is a zero-sum transfer of wealth through chance rather than labor, purchase, gift, loan or inheritance, rooted in the covetousness condemned by the Tenth Commandment, and cites a parent whose 20-year-old son plays college football and says his teammates are "obsessed" with gambling apps. The piece calls on churches to recover a clear witness against gambling rather than treating it, as many evangelicals now do, as morally equivalent to buying a movie ticket.

TruthScript

Justice Samuel Alito's new collection of speeches shows what genuine judicial conservatism looks like at a moment when the political right is increasingly shaped by disruptive, revolutionary figures

Reviewing Alito's "So Ordered: An Originalist's View of the Constitution, the Court, and Our Country," the piece traces Alito's path from a working-class, Italian-immigrant household in Trenton through Princeton, Yale Law School and decades on the bench, arguing his reserved, text-and-tradition-bound style contrasts with both liberal judicial activism and the more disruptive instincts now found elsewhere on the right. It highlights Alito's dissent in Obergefell v. Hodges warning that traditional marriage views would become civilly disfavored, his majority opinion in Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization overturning Roe v. Wade, and his lone dissents in Snyder v. Phelps and United States v. Stevens defending limits on speech that enables cruelty. The review concludes Alito's quiet, disciplined originalism — keeping constitutional interpretation "grounded in history and everyday experience" rather than personal preference — represents a conservatism genuinely interested in conserving rather than disrupting.
